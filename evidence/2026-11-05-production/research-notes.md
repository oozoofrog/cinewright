## Film

- Alfred Hitchcock, **Alfred Hitchcock: my own methods**, BFI / Sight and Sound: https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/features/alfred-hitchcock-my-own-methods
- Verified location: the *Sabotage* dinner/knife discussion immediately before and under **Getting the audience into the picture** (no page numbers). Hitchcock describes cutting between hand, eyes, knife, and reaction rather than relying on exaggerated facial expression. He also describes holding one person's image under another's voice. These are his methods, not universal laws or a prohibition of continuous shots.
- Proposed adaptation: make the order of attention and causal inference visible through cropped drawings and cut order. Preserve a required visible action. Check whether the important object and reaction connect without explanatory notes. Asset requirements: shared drawing, detail crop or reaction drawing; implementation: exposure/value plus anchor and cut compiler.

## Comics

- Jessica Abel and Matt Madden, **Student guide chapter 4: Bridging The Gap**, Drawing Words & Writing Pictures: https://dw-wp.com/2010/03/chapter-4-bridging-the-gap/
- Verified locations: opening explanation of closure; **Prep questions** (time between panels, depicted subject, scene changes); **Homework → Closure comics**; **Extra credit / Resources → Five-card Nancy**. The guide explicitly attributes closure to Scott McCloud. We inspected this authors' guide, not McCloud's complete book.
- Proposed adaptation: use before/after stills and held negative space to invite inference when intermediate movement is not mandatory. Maintain sufficient direction/object/identity clues. Check causal ambiguity and whether an omitted required action has actually disappeared. Avoid equating comic gutter width with a fixed number of animation frames.

## Philosophy

- Henri Bergson, **Time and Free Will**, translated by F. L. Pogson, Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56852/56852-h/56852-h.htm
- Verified location: Chapter II, printed pp. 100–106; HTML page markers and text were inspected. The argument distinguishes measurable juxtaposition from qualitative duration, using successive notes that organize into a whole as an example.
- Proposed adaptation: judge a held image in the sequence it follows and the expectation it creates; compare entire alternatives in their own timing. This is a philosophical prompt for production judgment, not a verified perceptual/neuroscience rule and not a reason to abandon integer frame timing.

## Visual art

- MoMA, Glenn Lowry / conservator Karl Buchberg / curator Jodi Hauptman, **Henri Matisse. Composition, Black and Red (Composition, noir et rouge). 1947**, *Henri Matisse: The Cut-Outs*, audio transcript 7/18: https://www.moma.org/audio/playlist/6/307
- Verified location: transcript under the work title, especially Buchberg's positive/negative paper shapes. The discussion shows how both the cut shape and its surround function in the composition.
- Proposed adaptation: inspect figure and remaining empty field together. Use silhouette, contrast, crop, and spacing to guide attention; do not fill intentional emptiness with motion by default. Check form readability at actual viewing size, clipping, and whether a light/texture layer overwhelms the intended focal shape. These animation choices are ours, not assertions that MoMA prescribes timing rules.

## Theatre

- Royal Shakespeare Company, **Example Activities for the Classroom**: https://www.rsc.org.uk/learn/schools-and-teachers/teacher-resources/rehearsal-room-approaches-to-shakespeare/example-activities-for-the-classroom
- Verified location: **3. Discovering the characters → Sculpting**. Poses, group placement, and freeze frames are used to explore character relations and allegiances.
- Additional primary institutional material: RSC, **Macbeth teacher pack (2011)**, **ACTIVITY: Instant images**, printed p. 5; **ACTIVITY: Emotion Imaging** near the end (do not invent a page number for this part): https://cdn2.rsc.org.uk/sitefinity/education-pdfs/teacher-packs/edu-macbeth-teacherpack-2011.pdf?sfvrsn=2
- Original text describes comparing frozen relationships, adding language and reassessing them, and using physical proximity to express emotional strength. Proposed adaptation: transfer the scene function of focus/relationship change to drawing arrangement, object spacing, light, or offscreen sound. Do not literally reproduce a stage actor or omit mandated visible gestures. Inspect direction, distance, occlusion, and contact as seen, not as explained. Light is an available adaptation; do not falsely attribute a lighting rule to this source.

## Musical theatre (separate from theatre)

- Mark Horowitz, **Isn't It Rich?**, *Library of Congress Magazine*, September/October 2025, printed p. 19: https://loc.gov/lcm/pdf/LCM_2025_0910.pdf
- Inspected original PDF text and printed-page label. The song's focal character changed during rehearsal; short phrases related to Glynis Johns's vocal capacity; surviving sketches include character subtext. Paraphrase without reproducing lyrics. Adaptation: organize image changes around dramatic purpose, phrase, breath/rest, and a change of thought rather than every beat. A source about one song does not impose a universal method.
- Ayse Tashkiran (author/interviewer), **Movement Directors in Contemporary Theatre: Conversations on Craft**, Methuen Drama, 2020, Chapter 10 **Peter Darling**, 2018 interview, printed pp. 129–130 (PDF indices 141–142): https://crco.cssd.ac.uk/id/eprint/1717/1/9781350054479_web.pdf#page=142
- Verified original interview: Darling aims to keep storytelling going through musical numbers; reads lyrics for situation, character objective, and action; movement should relate to the subject rather than decorate it. Adaptation: when the image becomes rhythmic, its event or relationship must still develop. No mandatory dancing, constant movement, or actor realism.
- Nathan Beary Blustein, **“Meet the Blob”: Solo-to-Ensemble “Reverse Reprises” in Merrily We Roll Along**, March 2018 conference abstract, and **Playwriting in Song: “Reprise types” in Stephen Sondheim's Sweeney Todd**, 2016 abstract, author's university research page: https://edspace.american.edu/nblustein/research/
- Original research abstract inspected. He analyzes recurring music acquiring different dramatic meaning and changes in music/lyrics/context. This is the researcher's analysis, not a verified quotation from Sondheim's book. Adaptation: a repeated still/motif can carry changed meaning after an event; check whether the new context is visible rather than merely replaying the same image. Cite this as analysis and use only if useful.
